[The Candy House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Candy_House_(novel)) is a novel by Jennifer Egan. It follows the lives of several interconnected characters. Although the novel contains multiple scenes set in the past, the present is in the near future (the 2030s) in a world where technology has evolved to allow people to upload their consciousness and past memories into the internet (n.b. that the technology is named Own Your Unconscious), which also allows others to experience the memories of those who have uploaded them.

The novel is essentially a compilation of stories about several characters told over the course of several decades. The reader can construct an overarching narrative from the way in which these stories touch one another. For instance, in one story, a teenage version of Molly Cooke meets a young Lulu at her country club and has a memorable afternoon with her and some older boys. In another story, we follow Lulu, who has become a spy. And in yet another, we read emails from Lulu, who is working with Ames Hollander to scan her body for a recording device she believes is still implanted in her (from her days as a spy). The novel tells several stories in this fashion, where the protagonist of one story appears as a secondary or tertiary character in another story. 

In some of the stories, Egan addresses the ethics, psychology, aesthetics, what have you, of the Own Your Unconscious and the Mandala company fairly directly. For instance, in one, Roxy Kline -- a recovering heroin addict -- buys a Mandala cube and uploads her memories into the cloud. She's excited about the prospect of doing so, and sees this as a key moment in her recovery. Other stories, such as the one following the teenage Molly Cooke, is not *about* the Own Your Unconsciousness technology at all; rather, it's a character study into the life of a teenage girl, and it sets up connections between Molly, Chris Salazar, and Lulu Kisarian, who are all important to the themes of the novel.

Overall, I mostly liked the book. The line-by-line writing is great. Some of the connections between the stories felt a bit oblique at times, but this may have been more a symptom of my reading than an issue with the book itself. That is, I may have made stronger connections had a read more carefully and diligently. I do appreciate that, unlike a novel such as *The Circle* by Dave Eggers, Egan has a much lighter touch and explores both the benefits and dangers of the Own Your Unconsciousness technology, whereas *The Circle* is more overtly moralistic against Big Tech.
